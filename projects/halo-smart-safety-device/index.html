Project Case Study

Halo Smart Safety Device

A portable safety device that combined calibrated sensing, Bluetooth alerts, and structured team execution.

Halo was developed as a senior design project focused on drink-spiking prevention. The core idea was to build a battery-powered protective cover that could fit common cup types, detect suspicious cover removal, and notify the user through a connected phone before the drink was left exposed.

What made the project meaningful was that it had to work as both a product and a sensing system. The device used an Arduino Nano 33 BLE, a strain gauge, and onboard motion sensing to distinguish real tampering from ordinary movement. That meant the design effort was not only about building a prototype, but about making detection logic reliable enough to reduce false positives while still keeping the device portable, lightweight, and easy to place on a drink in real settings.

My role was central both technically and organizationally. I designed and led development of the device, helped guide a five-person team through the second half of the academic year, and coordinated work in Trello while documenting progress through biweekly logs. On the engineering side, I implemented calibrated Arduino code, integrated BLE communication, contributed to the iOS app, and carried out hardware testing, soldering, prototype construction, and sensor calibration. The final system reached 95% detection accuracy while maintaining a clear smartphone alert flow.

Highlights

  • Leadership: Led a five-person team, tracked execution in Trello, prepared formal reports, and presented progress to faculty and the project advisor.
  • Detection system: Combined calibrated strain-gauge sensing with accelerometer-based motion data to better separate actual tampering from normal cup movement.
  • Connected experience: Implemented BLE communication and contributed to the iOS app so the device could show live status and send smartphone alerts when cover removal was detected.
  • Validation: Achieved 95% detection accuracy, giving the prototype more credibility as a functional safety product instead of a purely conceptual demo.
  • Engineering tradeoffs: Worked through fragile strain gauges, inconsistent amplifier behavior, physical assembly constraints, and the tension between portability, reliability, and future manufacturability.

Why It Mattered

Halo was compelling because it sat at the intersection of hardware reliability, user trust, and team execution. A safety product like this cannot just be technically interesting; it has to feel dependable, portable, and intuitive enough that someone would actually carry it and use it. That made the project as much about disciplined product thinking and communication as about sensing hardware.

Just as important, the project required formal engineering communication: technical reports, organized GitHub repositories, faculty presentations, and a documented testing process. That made it a strong example of leading a system from concept to prototype while keeping the work structured enough for others to build on.
